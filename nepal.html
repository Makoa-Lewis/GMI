The Sarangi Documentation Project · Nepal · 2027

Preserving the Voice of the Himalayas: The Sarangi & Gandharva Archives

Songs that carry a people's memory. A collaborative archive of the Gandharva sarangi tradition, made alongside the community that keeps it alive.

From late April to mid-June 2027, a GMI student cohort will join our partners in Nepal to record the living oral storytelling tradition of Gandharva musicians in rural Himalayan communities. Together we will build an audio, video, photographic, and written archive, created with community consent and shared back with the people whose music it is.

The living tradition

The Gandharvas and the sarangi.

For centuries, Gandharva musicians, historically known as the Gaine, have walked from village to village across Nepal with a sarangi in hand, carrying the news, history, and stories of the nation in song.

The sarangi is a four-string bowed instrument carved from a single block of wood, its hollowed body shaped by hand. In the hands of a Gandharva musician, it accompanies karkhas, heroic ballads that preserve the deeds of real people, along with seasonal songs and everyday stories. Before radio and newspapers reached the hills, Gandharva singers were Nepal's news carriers and oral historians.

The Gandharva community has safeguarded this tradition for generations, often in the face of caste discrimination. It belongs to them.

Today the tradition is both thriving and changing. In Kathmandu, a new generation is carrying the sarangi onto stages around the world, while in rural communities, much of the older repertoire lives in memory and performance. Our role is to listen, to learn, and to help create a lasting record, on the community's terms.

The living guardians

The Gandharva community

The musicians, families, and elders who have kept this tradition alive as Nepal's storytellers, news carriers, and oral historians. Every recording in this project belongs first to them.

Pioneering revival

Kiran Nepali and Project Sarangi

Founded in Kathmandu in 2012, Project Sarangi has helped bring the sarangi to a new generation: teaching young musicians, hand-crafting instruments in Kirtipur, contemporizing the sound, and hosting Jamarko, a free annual celebration of Nepali folk instruments.

Project Sarangi on YouTube
Four generations

Sarangi Ghar: Shyam Nepali and Prince Nepali

Founded by Shyam Nepali and his son Prince Nepali, Sarangi Ghar carries four generations of Gandharva musical mastery, from Magar Gaine and Ram Sharan Nepali to today, through teaching, performance, instrument craft, and collaborations around the world.

A historic voice

Jhalak Man Gandharva (1935–2003)

The first Gandharva singer to record Gaine songs, Jhalak Man Gandharva brought the tradition to listeners across Nepal through Radio Nepal and songs like "Aamale Sodhlin Ni," and revived the karkha form for a new era.

GMI is not affiliated with Project Sarangi or Sarangi Ghar. We credit them because their work is part of the living story of the sarangi, and we hope to learn from them.

The 2027 fieldwork

What we will make together.

Every recording is made with the informed consent of the musicians, and the community keeps ownership of its stories. Copies of the archive return to the community first.

A shared archive

Audio, video, photographs, and written documentation of performances, karkhas, and interviews, preserved for the community, for researchers, and for future musicians.

A documentary film

A substantial documentary that lets the musicians tell their own stories, in their own words, to audiences around the world.

Educational materials

Resources for classrooms and young musicians, in Nepal and beyond, so the songs can be learned and passed on.

Open doors

Why this matters to GMI.

GMI exists to expand access to music for young people in rural and underserved communities. The Gandharva tradition shows what that access has always looked like at its best: music carried to places others overlooked, and knowledge passed from one generation to the next. What we learn in Nepal will shape our programs at home and help build a growing network of chapters and partner communities around the world. See our five-year plan →

  1. Fall 2026 – Spring 2027Planning with partners

    Funding, cohort selection, and consent and protocol set jointly with our partners in Nepal.

  2. Late April – mid-June 2027Fieldwork

    Relationship-building first, then recording, ending with a community showcase in the village.

  3. Summer – Oct 2027Post-production

    Editing the film and building the archive, with materials shared back to the community.

  4. Fall 2027Premiere

    A screening at Vanderbilt, then festival submissions including POINT OF VU, DOC NYC, and the Krakow Film Festival's DocFilmMusic competition.
