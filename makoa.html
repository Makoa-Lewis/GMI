Makoa Lewis · Founder, Global Music Initiative

No private lessons. At 15, a stage with the LA Phil.

Makoa Lewis grew up in rural Hockinson, Washington, worked kitchens and landscaping crews to pay his own way, and earned a place beside Gustavo Dudamel. Now he is building the Global Music Initiative so the next student with talent and no teacher doesn't have to do it alone.

His story

The doors didn't open for him. He reached for them.

01 · Hockinson, Washington

One band director. No teachers.

Makoa grew up in Hockinson, a rural, low-income community in southwest Washington. At his high school, music came down to one person. A single band director ran the wind bands and the jazz band. There were no instrumental instructors, few working musicians nearby, and private lessons most families couldn't afford. Most students never took one. Makoa didn't either, not until he got to Vanderbilt.

As a freshman, a teacher told him he could see what Makoa was capable of. All he had to do was reach out far enough to grab it.

02 · The drive

So he reached.

He found BRAVO, an El Sistema–inspired youth program, and drove himself there. He paid for the car, the insurance and the gas on his own, working in fast-food kitchens and on landscaping crews through high school while keeping up with classes and a demanding rehearsal schedule.

In his words, he worked harder than any student should have to. And as he did, more doors opened.

03 · The world stage

From a rural band room to the LA Phil.

At 15 he was accepted to the YOLA National Festival and performed alongside the LA Phil and Gustavo Dudamel. He went on to spend three years with Youth Orchestra Los Angeles, a program that was completely free for him and the students it serves.

He was chosen as one of only 10 students in the United States to represent the country in the Pacific Honors Ensemble in Brisbane, Australia. He played with the El Sistema YOLA Orchestra Collective in Toronto, and performed percussion with the Orquesta Sinfónica Nacional de Paraguay in the Paraguayan premiere of Mahler's Symphony No. 2.

04 · The realization

Talent is everywhere. Access isn't.

What stayed with him wasn't only the stage. It was the young musicians around him, from towns and neighborhoods he had never heard of, who would never have met if someone hadn't made a deliberate effort to put them in the same room. And it was knowing how easily he might never have been in that room at all.

05 · Vanderbilt and GMI

Now he's holding the doors open.

Today Makoa is a QuestBridge Scholar at Vanderbilt University, Class of 2029. He started as a performance major. After a hand injury, he rebuilt his plan instead of abandoning it, and now majors in Human and Organizational Development and in Culture, Advocacy, and Leadership, with a minor in Percussion Performance. He is a member of the Vanderbilt Percussion Group and toured Portugal with it in spring 2026.

Through Immersion Vanderbilt he developed the Global Music Initiative. Its mission is opportunity, not just lessons or instruments. However a student needs help, GMI provides it. He is leading its first project, the Sarangi Documentation Project in Nepal, with Karun Dhakal and The Initiatives Foundation.

In his words

Why I started GMI.

I didn't grow up near a concert hall. I grew up in a small town where most kids never got the chance to find out what they could do with music.

When I was a freshman, a teacher told me he saw what I was capable of, and that I only needed to reach out far enough to grab it. I worked harder than any student should have to. And the more I reached, the more doors opened.

When I was fifteen, I got into the YOLA National Festival and ended up on stage with the LA Phil and Gustavo Dudamel. The performance changed me, but the people changed me more. I was surrounded by young musicians from towns and neighborhoods I'd never heard of. We came from completely different places, and most of us would never have met any other way. Someone had made a deliberate effort to put us in the same room.

That's what GMI is built on. Talent and curiosity are everywhere. Opportunity isn't. I want to build the kind of doors that were opened for me, and hold them open for as many people as possible.

Nepal is where we start. Gandharva musicians have carried stories on the sarangi for generations, and much of that tradition has never been fully recorded. I want us to document it with the musicians who carry it, respectfully and with our partners in Nepal, so it can be heard and passed on.

I also want to bring a cohort of students who, like me, might never have been in that room otherwise. Students from different schools, backgrounds, and fields, working side by side. Cost shouldn't decide who gets to go.

Nepal is only the beginning. If we get this right, it becomes the first link in a growing network of university chapters and communities around the world, preserving music, connecting people who would never have met, and proving that a small, determined group of students can do work that matters.
